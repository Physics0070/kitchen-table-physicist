    How it works

    Five steps. No formula handed in.

    1. 01

      Look

      A vision model watches the clip and decides what is worth tracking, and which object in frame has a size it knows.

    2. 02

      Track

      Segmentation or a coloured sticker follows the object frame by frame. A sheet of A4 turns pixels into metres.

    3. 03

      Discover

      A search builds laws from generic pieces, guided by the data's own mean, slope and rhythm. Each law is fitted on the first 70% of the clip and scored on the 30% it never saw.

    4. 04

      Try to break it

      The winning law is refitted on each half of the clip. A true law keeps its constants. One that drifts is marked incomplete.

    5. 05

      Remember

      Every law goes into a library. A later clip that disagrees with an old law marks it broken.

    The self-test

    Try to break its law yourself.

    This is the app's own pendulum. Its law predicted the unseen frames to half a millimetre. A true law keeps its constants on any slice of the clip. Hold the button to fit the second half on its own and see where its swing frequency lands.

    Questions judges ask

    The four we hear most.

    Is anything hardcoded?
    No formula families and no list of experiments. The search composes laws from constants, powers of time, waves and decays, and the model can propose any other formula on top. On the drop it tried 67 laws and kept a 2-constant one.
    Doesn't the model just recite textbook formulas?
    It can't win that way. Every law is scored in code on frames it never saw. On the drop, a line plus a wave came within 1.8 mm using 5 constants. The t² law did better, 1.4 mm, with 2.
    Is running model-written maths safe?
    Each formula is parsed and checked before it runs: arithmetic and a short list of functions only, no attribute access, no builtins, numbers forced to floats so a huge power fails fast. An independent review tried to break it. The two holes it found are closed and tested.
    Has it run on real footage?
    Not yet. Everything on this page comes from rendered clips with known physics, which is exactly why the answers can be checked. A real phone clip of a pendulum is the next step.
